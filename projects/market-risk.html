Stress Testing and Market Risk

Four Value-at-Risk models backtested on 35 years of daily data, and seven historical crises replayed on today's portfolio. Everything here runs on public data and open-source Python.

View the Code on GitHub Backtest Explorer Stress Calculator Results by Ticker

A sterling investor holds £10m of US equities, Treasuries, corporate bonds and oil. How much could the portfolio lose tomorrow, and how would it fare in a repeat of 2008? The project answers both questions with four VaR models, formal backtests over 8,912 trading days and seven historical stress scenarios.

The Portfolio

Every position is held at a constant value in sterling and rebalanced daily, so the book carries equity, rates, credit, commodity and currency risk at the same time.

Data

The data are five daily series from FRED, the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis database, running from May 1987 to October 2026. They are stored in a DuckDB database and queried with SQL. The series do not share a calendar: Brent prices are missing on 986 of 9,941 NASDAQ trading days. Keeping only the dates on which every series has data would silently turn some one-day returns into returns over a week. The data layer therefore uses NASDAQ trading days as the calendar and carries the other series forward over at most five missing days.

Methods

ModelWindowIdea
Historical simulation500 daysTomorrow looks like a random day from the last two years
Normal500 daysA normal distribution with the window's mean and volatility
EWMA (RiskMetrics)500 days, λ = 0.94Recent days weigh more, so volatility reacts within days
Filtered historical simulation1,000 daysGARCH(1,1) volatility rescales past shocks to tomorrow's conditions, keeping their fat tails

Each model forecasts tomorrow's VaR and Expected Shortfall at 99% and 97.5% using only data up to today. The forecasts are then judged against what happened:

  • Kupiec test: is the share of exceptions equal to 1% (or 2.5%)?
  • Christoffersen test: are exceptions independent of each other, or do they cluster?
  • Basel traffic light: how many exceptions fell in the trailing 250 days (green 0–4, yellow 5–9, red 10 or more)?
  • ES ratio: on exception days, how did the actual loss compare with the forecast Expected Shortfall?

The code is a Python package with an object-oriented design. Every VaR model inherits from one abstract base class, every instrument from a Position class, and every stress shock from a Shock class, so a new model or instrument plugs into the backtester without changing it. The package has 38 unit tests, run by GitHub Actions on every push. This page is plain JavaScript reading a JSON file that the package exports.

What It Shows

  • No model passes at 99%. All four have 30–50% more exceptions than expected, and the exceptions cluster. The portfolio's tails are fatter, and its volatility more persistent, than any model assumes.
  • The filtered model is the best calibrated. Its Expected Shortfall is right on average (an ES ratio of 1.00), and at 97.5%, the confidence level of the Basel FRTB Expected Shortfall, it has 221 exceptions against 222.8 expected.
  • Historical simulation is slow to react. In 2008 it had 15 exceptions at 99%, against 7 for the filtered model, because its two-year window still held the calm of 2006–07.
  • A falling pound is a hedge for this portfolio. In 2022 US 10-year yields rose by 248 basis points, yet the Treasury position lost almost nothing in sterling, because the pound fell 17% against the dollar. In the UK mini-budget of September 2022 the portfolio gained.
  • A repeat of 2008 would cost seven days of VaR. Replayed on today's portfolio, the Lehman month loses £1.2m, 7.0 times today's one-day 99% VaR.

Limitations

  • The P&L is hypothetical: values are held constant, so the backtest judges the models, not trading decisions.
  • Bond returns come from constant-maturity yields; the Baa yield averages bonds of 20 years and longer.
  • Equities and oil use spot data, with no dividends, futures roll or funding cost.
  • Stress scenarios apply a whole window's moves at once, with no rebalancing or liquidity cost along the way.
